# Research Communication and Presentation

This page helps you prepare clear, accessible, and transparent outputs for your EXPRA project—your **poster**, **manuscript**, and any **oral presentation**. It focuses on both scientific rigour and effective storytelling, so that your work communicates its content convincingly and ethically.

## Why Research Communication Matters

1.	**Good communication is integral to good science.** The most important point is to present your findings in a clear manner, as this ensures that others can understand, evaluate, criticize and/or build on your work. In EXPRA, your poster and presentation are part of your open science workflow.
2.	**Good communication helps you being a good scientist.** When you strive to communicate clearly, you necessarily reflect on your results and their limitations.
3.	**Good communication helps others being good scientists.** A clear paper/poster will make research reproducible and transparent, engaging audiences beyond one’s own team. 
4.	**Good communication is fair.** Give proper credit to contributors and participants.

## Core Principles
1.	**Clarity** – Explain your question, method, and results in a plain, concise language.
2.	**Transparency** – Include preregistration, open data/code, and deviations from your plan.
3.	**Accuracy** – Present numbers and visuals honestly, without distorting data. Be careful about what (pre) processing does to your data (e.g., log-transformation for response times), and openly use it only if necessary.
4.	**Accessibility** – Use readable fonts, good contrast, and be mindful of color-impaired individuals when creating visual aids.
5.	**Acknowledgement** – Credit everyone who contributed or provided data/materials, and specify their role in the research process.

## Poster Guidelines
Check the subchapter {doc}`poster` for more details.

**1) Structure**
A clear poster usually follows this order:
1.	**Title and author information**
2.	**Research question and hypotheses**
3.	**Method** – participants, materials, procedure, analyses
4.	**Results** – key figures/tables with clear labels
5.	**Discussion** – interpretation, limitations, implications
6.	**Open science statements** – data, code, and materials availability
7.	**Acknowledgements** and contact information

**2) Design Tips**
•	Use **minimal text**; aim for bullet points and short sentences.
•	Maintain a **logical visual flow** (typically left-to-right, top-to-bottom).
•	Use **high contrast** and a readable font size (≥24pt for body text).
•	Avoid clutter—white space improves comprehension.
•	Choose color palettes accessible to color-blind individuals (e.g., https://davidmathlogic.com/colorblind/#%23D81B60-%231E88E5-%23FFC107-%23004D40).
•	Figure text should be legible.

**3) Accessibility Checklist**
•	Avoid red–green contrasts.
•	Ensure charts are coherently labelled with both colour and text.

## Oral Presentation
For more details, check the subchapter {doc}`presentation`.

**1) Focus on Storytelling**

Once you investigated your hypothesis and have beautiful figures and a poster (and **only** after that), frame your talk as a narrative: 
- **Setup** – What problem did you investigate? Why does it matter?
- **Approach** – How did you study it?
- **Findings** – What did you learn?
- **Meaning** – Why is this result important?

**2) Keep it Engaging**
•	Speak slowly and clearly; pause between points.
•	Use visuals to support, not duplicate, your speech.
•	Avoid reading slides verbatim.
•	Rehearse timing (6–8 minutes are typical for EXPRA presentations).

**3) Slide Design**
•	If you plan to have slides alongside your poster, put just one key idea per slide.
•	Avoid dense text or tables.
•	Use consistent fonts and colour schemes.
•	Include your **LIFOS**, **OSF or GitHub link** on the final slide.

**4) Questions and Discussion**
•	Anticipate typical questions (method alternatives, analysis choices, generalizability of your findings).
•	It is fine to say “That is a next step.”
•	Thank questioners.

## Report Writing
For more details, check the subchapter {doc}`writing`.

Your final written summary should mirror standard scientific structure:

**Introduction → Method → Results → Discussion**

### Writing Tips
•	Write in **plain, precise English**; avoid unnecessary jargon.
•	Define all abbreviations and statistical terms.
•	Report exact **p-values** (do not report p = 0), **effect sizes**, and **confidence intervals**.
•	Describe any **deviations from preregistration** clearly.
•	Include an **Open Science statement** at the end (see below).

### Example Open Science Statement
This study was preregistered at [LIFOS link]. Data, analysis scripts, and materials are available at [repository link]. Due to GDPR constraints, raw data cannot be shared, but a de-identified dataset and full code are/can be provided.

### Visual Aids
•	Use figures to show patterns, not just significance.
•	Label axes clearly with units.
•	Avoid misleading scales (start axes at 0 where meaningful).
•	Always include error bars.
•	Keep colour palettes consistent across figures.

### Referencing & Citation
•	Follow **APA 7th edition** for all EXPRA projects unless otherwise stated.
•	Use a reference manager (e.g., **Zotero**).

## Data and Code Availability
Be explicit in your outputs: 
- **Data availability statement** – where the dataset can be accessed, under what licence. -
- **Code availability statement** – where analysis scripts are stored.
- **Materials statement** – where questionnaires or stimuli are archived.

Refer to your LIFOS project (or GitHub repository, if you have one).

## Integration with Open Science
This section connects directly to the Open Science Principles and Practices page: 
- Use the same repository links and licences.
- Update your preregistration to include communication plans.
- Ensure consistency between preregistered analyses and reported results.
